| Step | Lens Card | Live Action |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | *Understand your interests* | Over two weeks Claude logs moments of absorption: debugging a friend's laptop at 1 a.m., reading about a data breach, explaining code to a junior. He circles a pattern: **systems, puzzles, explaining**. |
| **2** | *Identify your strengths* | He asks three friends and Meera, "What do I do better than most people?" Meera texts: *"You stay calm when things break. Annoyingly calm."* |

| **7** | *Select a direction* | He writes on a fresh page: *"Direction (for now): cybersecurity — protecting ordinary people and small businesses. Later: AI security?"* |

**Camera:** Clean, purposeful movement. Step 6(c) is the emotional peak: a slow push on Claude's face lit by monitor glow, absorbed, the watch visible showing time flying (a time-lapse shadow moves across the wall).
**Motion graphics:** A progress path of 8 stepping stones across a river, which Claude's silhouette crosses one stone at a time.

**NARRATION** (spread across the montage):
> **(Step 1)** Start with evidence, not fantasy. Not *"what sounds impressive"* but *"when do I actually lose track of time?"* Interests leave clues.
>
> **(Step 2)** Strengths are often invisible to the person who has them. They feel too easy to count. Ask the people who know you.
>
> **(Step 3)** Then look outward. Purpose rarely lives only inside your head. It lives where your abilities meet someone else's problem.
>
> **(Step 4)** Some skills open more doors than others: skills that solve expensive problems, that are hard to automate, and that compound with experience. Study what's valuable, not just what's trendy.
>
> **(Step 5)** Explore careers by their ordinary days, not their highlight reels. Ask what people actually *do* from nine to six.
>
> **(Step 6)** Then experiment. Small, cheap and fast. Most experiments will fail, and that's the point. A failed experiment that costs a week is far cheaper than a wrong path that costs a decade.
>
> *(beat — Step 6c)*
>
> And sometimes, in the middle of an ordinary task, something clicks. Time disappears. You feel useful *and* absorbed.
>
> **(Step 7)** Then choose. Not forever. Just a direction clear enough to say no to other things.
>
> **(Step 8)** And commit to consistent action. A direction without action is only a wish.

**NARRATION:**
> A popular way to think about this is a diagram often called *ikigai*. Four circles: what you love, what you are good at, what the world needs, and what people will pay for.
>
> Love without skill is a hobby. Skill without love can feel empty. Need without payment can burn you out. Payment without meaning can feel like selling hours.
>
> Where the circles overlap is a direction worth exploring.
>
> *(beat)*
>
> But a small honesty: this four-circle diagram is a modern, mostly Western adaptation. In Japan, the word *ikigai* is often used more simply, meaning a reason to get up in the morning. It might be a career. It might be a garden, a grandchild, a morning walk with friends.
>
> So use the diagram as a map, not a verdict. And remember that a life can have more than one reason for being.

## Key Lessons

1. **Purpose is answered by living**, through evidence and experiments, not one sudden revelation.
2. **The 8-step process:** interests → strengths → real problems → valuable skills → career exploration → small experiments → direction → consistent action.
3. **Look for absorption plus usefulness.** The moments where you lose track of time *and* help someone are strong clues.
4. **Ikigai (four circles) is a useful map, not a verdict.** It's a modern adaptation of a broader Japanese idea of "a reason for being", which can include non-career sources.
5. **Purpose evolves.** Earlier paths feed later ones.
6. **A real yes requires some no's.**

---

## Practical Exercises

1. **The Absorption Log (14 days).** Each evening note: *When did I lose track of time today? Was I also being useful to someone?* Look for patterns at the end.
2. **The Strength Survey.** Message 3–5 people who know you well: *"What's one thing I do better than most people?"* Write down their exact words. Look for repeats.
3. **Problem Walk.** Spend one hour noticing problems around you (in your neighbourhood, family, college or workplace). List 10. Circle the ones you could help with, now or with some training.
4. **Draw Your Four Circles.** Fill each circle with at least 5 items. Look for anything appearing in 3 or 4 circles. Treat these as *hypotheses*, not conclusions.
5. **Three Experiments in 30 Days.** Design three small, cheap tests of possible directions, each under 10 hours: a mini project, a conversation with a practitioner, a short course module. Afterwards, rate each from 1 to 5 for *energy* and *usefulness*.
6. **The One-Year Direction Statement.** Write: *"For the next 12 months, my direction is ___ because ___. I will review this on ___ (date)."*
7. **Add to the Manual:** Draft Rule #3: *your direction statement.*

## Chapter Summary

At twenty, under pressure to take the first safe job, Claude asks the oldest question: *What should I do with my life?* A librarian-mentor, Mr. Farhan, gives him questions instead of answers. Claude works through eight steps: logging interests, gathering honest feedback on strengths, walking his neighbourhood to find real problems, studying valuable skills, exploring careers by their ordinary days, and running cheap experiments. Then he chooses a direction and commits to consistent action. The **Ikigai** four-circle model maps where love, skill, need and payment meet, with an honest note that the diagram is a modern adaptation of a broader Japanese idea. Claude chooses a direction: protecting people from digital harm. He knows it can grow and change. **Purpose isn't found in a moment. It's built, tested, and allowed to grow.**
