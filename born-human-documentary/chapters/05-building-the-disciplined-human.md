> He started with the thing everything else depends on: sleep.
>
> Most adults need somewhere between seven and nine hours. Sleep is when the brain consolidates memory and the body repairs itself. >
> Morning light. Water. Then movement. Not punishment, just enough. Health guidelines suggest around two and a half hours of moderate activity a week, plus some strength work. Exercise benefits the heart and muscles, and research links it to better mood and thinking too.
>
> Then ten minutes of stillness. The same practice from the banyan tree. Not to empty the mind, but to start the day by noticing it.
>
> *(beat — Deep Work segment glows)*
>
> And then, the most valuable hours: deep work. The writer Cal Newport popularised the term for focused, distraction-free effort on cognitively demanding tasks. It's increasingly rare, which is exactly why it's valuable. Claude protected his best three hours for his hardest single task. Email could wait. It always could.
>
> Evenings had room for learning, for people, and for rest. And every night, the day ended with a review.
>
> *(beat)*
>
> It wasn't perfect. It wasn't rigid. It was a default — something to return to when the day went off course.

### Scene 5.5 — Cue → Routine → Reward (4:15–5:30)

**Visual:** The Lens. A **three-part loop** diagram (popularised by Charles Duhigg's *The Power of Habit*):

```
       CUE
      ↗    ↘
REWARD  ←  ROUTINE
```

**Example 1 (old habit):** Cue: *feeling stuck on a task.* Routine: *open phone.* Reward: *relief, novelty.* The loop spins fast and glows ochre.
**Example 2 (redesigned):** Same cue: *feeling stuck.* New routine: *stand up, refill water, write the next tiny step on paper.* Reward: *relief plus a small sense of progress.* The loop spins and glows teal.
Live action mirrors both versions in split-screen.
**Additional element:** Claude's habit tracker, a simple paper grid on the wall. X's accumulate. A note reads *"Never miss twice."*

**Motion graphics:** A small, honest stat card: *"In one study, forming a new automatic habit took a median of 66 days — ranging from 18 to 254 depending on the person and habit."* (Lally et al., 2010)

**NARRATION:**
> Habits run on a simple loop: a cue, a routine, a reward.
>
> You often can't remove the cue. Feeling stuck will happen. Boredom will happen. But you can change the routine that follows it, as long as the new routine still delivers some reward.
>
> And forming habits takes longer than the internet suggests. In one well-known study, it took a median of about 66 days for a new behaviour to feel automatic, with a huge range, from under three weeks to more than eight months.
>
> So Claude made one rule for the bad days: *never miss twice*. Missing once is life. Missing twice is the start of a new habit.

## Key Lessons

1. **Motivation fluctuates; systems persist.** Design for your tired, distracted self.
2. **Sleep is the foundation** (7–9 hours for most adults). Plan the day backward from bedtime.
3. **Exercise:** about 150–300 minutes of moderate activity weekly plus strength work twice a week (WHO guidelines).
4. **Protect deep work.** Your best hours go to your hardest task.
5. **Design your environment.** Visible means used and hidden means forgotten. Add friction to bad habits and remove it from good ones.
6. **Cue → Routine → Reward.** Keep the cue, change the routine and preserve a reward.
7. **Habits take time.** A median of around 66 days in one study, with wide variation. *Never miss twice.*
8. **Daily review is the habit that improves the other habits.**
9. **Small actions compound** in shape, if not by an exact formula.

## Practical Exercises

1. **Draw Your Real 24-Hour Clock.** For 3 days, log what you actually do in 30-minute blocks. Draw it as a circle. Then draw your *ideal default day* next to it. Choose **one** segment to change first.
2. **Sleep Anchor.** Pick a fixed wake-up time for 14 days, including weekends (allow up to 1 hour of variance). Count back 8 hours for a target bedtime. The phone charges outside the bedroom.
3. **Two-Minute Version.** Shrink each new habit to a version that takes under 2 minutes ("put on running shoes", "read one page", "open the file"). Do only that on hard days.
4. **Habit Loop Redesign.** Pick one unwanted habit. Write the cue, routine and reward. Design a replacement routine that answers the same cue and gives a similar reward. Test it for 2 weeks.
5. **Deep Work Block.** Schedule one 90-minute block, 3 days this week, for a single important task. Put the phone in another room and close all unrelated tabs. Log what you produce.
6. **The 10-Minute Evening Review.** Use the template from Scene 5.7 for 30 days. At the end, read all 30 entries in one sitting and look for patterns.
7. **Add to the Manual:** Draft Rule #4: *"Never miss twice. Return to the system."*

## Chapter Summary

After five failed fresh starts powered by motivation alone, Claude, now working as a junior security analyst, learns that discipline is a system, not a mood. He designs a **24-hour default day** around sleep (7–9 hours), movement, a short meditation, a protected deep work block, learning, people and rest, ending each day with a 10-minute review. He reshapes his environment so good choices are easy and bad ones harder. Using the **Cue → Routine → Reward** loop, he replaces bad habits instead of just resisting them. He learns that habits take weeks or months to form and adopts the rule *never miss twice*. Over a year, hundreds of ordinary days compound into real change. **Discipline is not a mood. It's a system you return to.**
